# Evidence and kinds of claims

Gourmet Gastronomer’s intellectual character depends on knowing **what kind of statement** is on the page.

Do not flatten everything into “facts with citations” or “vibes without them.” Distinguish:

| Kind | Meaning | How to write it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fact** | A determinate claim that is true or false independent of taste | State plainly; source it if it is not elementary |
| **Evidence** | The record that supports a fact | Cite the record; do not pretend the citation *is* the fact |
| **Inference** | A conclusion you draw from evidence | Signal the leap (“this suggests,” “a practical reading is”) |
| **Practitioner consensus** | A method widely used by competent professionals | Name it as workshop/bakery practice, not physics |
| **Editorial observation** | What a careful taster or cook can notice | Own it; do not dress it as a lab result |
| **Disputed claim** | Serious people disagree | Report the disagreement; do not pick a winner without evidence |
| **Tradition / folklore** | A story a culture tells about food | Present as tradition; never as automatic scientific explanation |
| **Uncertainty** | Not yet established | Leave the hole visible |

If you cannot tell which row a sentence belongs to, the sentence is not ready.

## Scientific / technical claims

Require strong technical evidence: peer-reviewed work, university or extension publications, standards methods, technical monographs, manufacturer documentation for equipment or ingredients.

Examples:

- Gluten network formation
- Pectin gel conditions
- Microbial genera in a ferment
- Water activity and spoilage
- Flour standards of identity

Do not upgrade a bakery blog into cereal chemistry. Discovery sources may *lead* you to a paper; they may not *anchor* the claim. See [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md) and [SOURCE-ECOSYSTEMS.md](SOURCE-ECOSYSTEMS.md).

## Food-safety claims

Require authoritative sources. Agents must **never improvise**:

- canning process times
- preservation safety thresholds
- pathogen-control claims
- storage limits presented as safety rules
- dairy safety claims
- fermentation safety parameters (pH, time, salt, temperature as control)

For U.S. home preserving, start at NCHFP and USDA-tested processes. For cheesemaking safety, prefer regulators, university dairy programs, and professional guidance such as ACS materials — still as guidance, not as a substitute for the applicable rule.

If you cannot source a safety number, **omit the number**. Do not estimate. Do not “round” a process. Do not invent a rescue for a botulism-relevant failure.

Quality advice (“the jam is ugly but the sealed, tested process was followed”) is not a safety waiver.

## Historical claims

Need historical or primary support: archives, dated journalism, scholarly history, producer records, legal filings, contemporary accounts.

Do not date a bakery, a style, or a neighborhood reputation from memory. San Francisco sourdough is a real historical and microbiological subject; it is also a magnet for folklore. Keep those layers separate.

## Business / producer claims

Prefer the producer’s own current site or filings for claims about *themselves* (address, ownership, what they say they make). Use reliable reporting for claims about *reception*, labor, or controversy.

Do not launder marketing language as encyclopedia voice. Hours and addresses go stale; verify or omit.

## Sensory observations

Can legitimately be editorial if presented that way:

> The crumb smells more yogurt-like than vinegar-like when lactic activity dominates.

That is not a citation-shaped fact. It is a trained observation. Do not fake a study for it. Do not present it as universal.

## Recommendations

Must be framed as judgment rather than universal law:

> For a mixed-flour hearth loaf in a home kitchen, many bakers aim near 24–26 °C after mixing — a production target, not a moral rule.

A recommendation without a stated context is a slogan.

## Tradition / folklore

Should not automatically be presented as scientific explanation.

“Leaves in the barrel” or “never use metal” or “sourdough is more digestible” may be tradition, marketing, or a hypothesis. Say which. If a study exists, cite the study and its limits. If it does not, do not smuggle folklore into the mechanism section.

## What agents must not do

- Invent quotations, URLs, ISBNs, DOIs, or page numbers
- Cite a source that does not support the sentence
- Fill frontmatter `sources:` to look complete
- Treat social media consensus as microbiology
- Resolve a dispute by tone

When evidence is thin, write a shorter page. Silence is more honest than a decorative bibliography.

## Using this in prose

You do not need labels on every sentence. You need discipline:

- Safety numbers → named authority
- Mechanisms → technical source or an explicit inference
- Shop talk → “bakers often,” with a decent source when the method is specific
- Tasting notes → first-person-plural or plainly observational
- History → dated evidence
- Folklore → named as such
