Acidity is sourness you can taste and pH you can measure. They are related, not identical. Organic acids (lactic, acetic, tartaric, citric, malic) are different molecules with different smells and different jobs.

## In fermentation

[[gg:topic:lactic-acid-bacteria]] make lactic acid, and often acetic acid, in dough and in milk. That is why sourdough can taste yogurt-like or vinegar-like depending on temperature and hydration — a baking observation, not a city name. Cheese cultures acidify milk so casein will set; FAO’s acid coagulation is a pH-and-temperature problem [src:gg:source:fao-small-scale-cheese]. Wine’s tartaric acid is largely from the [[gg:ingredient:grape]]; fermentation also produces acids. The shared idea is [[gg:topic:fermentation]]; the crafts apply it.

## In jam

Pectin gels need acid in a workable window. Extension: when a recipe calls for lemon juice, it is for gel formation [src:gg:source:ohioline-hyg-5350]. Too little acid, weak gel. Too much, a gel that weeps. See [[gg:topic:jam]] and [[gg:topic:pectin]].

## In wine

Acid keeps wine from tasting flat and increases how astringent [[gg:topic:tannin]] feels [src:gg:source:smith-tannin-ajgw-2015]. Taste it as watering of the mouth and a sharp edge, using [[gg:topic:wine-tasting]].

## In canning

Here acidity is a safety line, not a flavor note. NCHFP/USDA: foods with pH above 4.6 are low-acid; botulinum spores are not reliably killed in a boiling-water canner; pressure canning is required. Acid foods at pH 4.6 or below may use boiling-water processes [src:gg:source:nchfp-ensuring-safe-canned-foods]. Do not “fix” a low-acid canned vegetable by adding a splash of vinegar unless you are on a tested pickled recipe. See [[gg:topic:canning]] and [[gg:topic:food-safety]].

Taste sourness. Measure pH when the decision is safety. Do not confuse those jobs.
