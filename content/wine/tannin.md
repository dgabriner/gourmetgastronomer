Tannin is why some wines make your mouth feel dry. It is not a flavor like cherry. It is a texture.

In wine, condensed tannins come mainly from grape skins, seeds, and to a lesser extent other tissues; oak can add a smaller amount of hydrolyzable tannin [src:gg:source:smith-tannin-ajgw-2015]. Astringency is the drying, puckering sensation from tannin binding and precipitating saliva proteins, so the mouth loses lubrication [src:gg:source:smith-tannin-ajgw-2015]. Bitterness is a taste; astringency is a feel. Small phenolics skew more bitter; larger polymers more astringent — a useful tendency, not a tasting-wheel law.

## Extraction is a winemaking decision

Red fermentation keeps juice in contact with skins. That maceration extracts anthocyanins (color) relatively early and tannins more as alcohol rises [src:gg:source:smith-tannin-ajgw-2015]. Extended maceration after the sugar is gone is a choice for more structure, not a moral improvement. Over-extraction tastes harsh. White wines usually avoid this on purpose.

Punch-downs, pump-overs, cap management, and temperature are the levers. They belong to production; you taste the result.

## Acidity changes the feel

The same tannin feels more astringent in a more acidic wine [src:gg:source:smith-tannin-ajgw-2015]. [[gg:topic:acidity]] is therefore part of tannin perception, not a separate optional topic. Sugar can mask; it does not delete the protein binding.

## Aging

In bottle or barrel, tannins keep reacting — with each other, with anthocyanins, with oxygen at a trickle. Color shifts; the grip often softens. That chemistry is sketched on [[gg:topic:wine-aging]]. It is not “the wine becomes more elegant” as an explanation.

Tea, unripe persimmon, and young Cabernet share the drying trick. [[gg:ingredient:grape]] variety, ripeness, and seeds versus skins change the starting mix. Taste it using [[gg:topic:wine-tasting]], not a score.
