Jam is fruit you can still see, cooked with [[gg:ingredient:sugar]] until a gel holds it. Ohio State’s kitchen definition: thick spreads from crushed or chopped fruit with sugar; they hold their shape but are generally less firm than jelly [src:gg:source:ohioline-hyg-5350]. That is culinary language. U.S. labeling has a separate standard of identity: a finished jam or preserve must reach at least 65% soluble solids, with a specified fruit-to-sugar ratio (47 parts fruit to 55 parts saccharine ingredient for Group I fruits such as strawberry; 45:55 otherwise) [src:gg:source:cfr-21-150-160-jam]. A delicious home pot can fail that legal name and still be jam in the kitchen. A commercial jar using the word on a label is playing a different game.

## How jam sets

A conventional high-methoxyl gel is a conversation among four things:

- **[[gg:topic:pectin]]** — the polysaccharide that can form a network.
- **[[gg:ingredient:sugar]]** — pulls water so pectin chains can find each other; also flavor and, in traditional formulas, microbial control.
- **[[gg:topic:acidity]]** — the pH window where that network forms. Extension: added lemon juice or citric acid is there for gel, not decoration [src:gg:source:ohioline-hyg-5350].
- **Concentration** — boiling drives off water. Under-concentrated jam is syrup. Over-concentrated jam is candy with fruit in it.

Low-methoxyl (“no sugar needed”) pectins gel with calcium and do not use the same sugar contract. Their packets are a different process. Do not swap them into a regular-pectin formula.

Strawberries are a teaching fruit because they are often low in pectin when fully ripe. OSU groups strawberries with fruits that always need added acid, pectin, or both [src:gg:source:ohioline-hyg-5350]. That is why the NCHFP strawberry formula stirs in powdered pectin *before* the sugar [src:gg:source:nchfp-strawberry-jam-pectin]. See [[gg:ingredient:strawberry]].

## Observation, not a magic thermometer

Clocks lie. Fruit pectin and water content vary.

Ohio State lists three doneness tests for cooked spreads [src:gg:source:ohioline-hyg-5350]:

- **Temperature.** At sea level, 220 °F is the traditional jelly thermometer cue, dropping as altitude rises because water boils cooler. Treat the number as a concentration hint, not a commandment. Pectin-added jams often finish by a timed hard boil instead.
- **Sheet or spoon.** Dip a cold metal spoon; jam is close when two drops run together and hang.
- **Cold plate.** A spoonful on a frozen plate wrinkles when pushed.

Watch the surface: a full rolling boil with bubbles across the kettle is not a simmer. Listen for the change from watery rattle to a thicker, slower boil. Smell the fruit concentrating — then smell scorch if you stopped stirring.

## Variables

Fruit variety and ripeness, whether you added commercial pectin, batch size (double batches often fail to gel), kettle shape, and how hard you boil. Extension: make one recipe at a time, no more than 6–8 cups of juice for jelly-scale work [src:gg:source:ohioline-hyg-5350].

## Failure modes

- **Weak gel.** Low pectin, low acid, low sugar, or not concentrated. Quality failure.
- **Weeping.** Too much acid, or a gel that was moved while setting. Quality failure.
- **Crystals.** Sugar not dissolved, or overcooking.
- **Unsafe jar.** A gel in a sealed jar that did not follow a tested canning process is not a pantry food. Jams as acid foods still need the researched process if they are to sit on a shelf [src:gg:source:nchfp-ensuring-safe-canned-foods]. Paraffin is not that process [src:gg:source:ohioline-hyg-5350].

## Making jam versus canning jam

Cooking to a set is a gel problem. [[gg:topic:canning]] is a heat-process problem. [[gg:recipe:strawberry-jam]] is NCHFP’s tested canned strawberry jam with powdered pectin, including 5 / 10 / 15 minute boiling-water times by altitude [src:gg:source:nchfp-strawberry-jam-pectin]. If you change the fruit, sugar, or acid, you have left that process. Refrigerate or freeze experiments; do not invent a water-bath time.
