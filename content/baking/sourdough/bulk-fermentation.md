Bulk fermentation is the period after [[gg:topic:mixing]] and before dividing, when the whole dough mass ferments as one piece. It is not the [[gg:topic:final-proof]] in a basket, and it is not a kitchen timer with a personality. Yeast and lactic acid bacteria from the [[gg:topic:sourdough-starter]] (or from baker’s yeast, in other doughs) produce gas, organic acids, and aroma while [[gg:topic:dough-development]] continues through rest and folds.

## Why it matters

What happens here sets how much gas the loaf will have to work with, how extensible the gluten is when you shape, and how pronounced the acid will be later. A loaf that is dense, exploding at the score, or collapsing in the oven is often a bulk problem that was merely *noticed* at bake.

There is no universal minute count. Temperature, inoculation, flour enzyme activity, and dough strength change the clock. The mix target that sets that clock is [[gg:topic:desired-dough-temperature]]. Calvel treats fermentation as a condition to observe, not a slogan [src:gg:source:calvel-taste-of-bread]. King Arthur’s desired-dough-temperature teaching makes the same practical point: let the dough’s progress guide you, not the clock [src:gg:source:king-arthur-desired-dough-temperature].

## Mechanism

[[gg:topic:fermentation]] in dough is gas plus acid plus flavor compounds, happening in a gluten network that is still being organized.

- **Gas.** Yeast produces carbon dioxide. Early in bulk the dough looks sleepy; later it is aerated throughout, not just puffy on top.
- **Acid and aroma.** Lactic acid bacteria acidify the dough and contribute the sour and dairy notes people associate with sourdough. Acid also tightens gluten over time. That is useful until it is too much.
- **Strength vs extensibility.** Folds reorganize gluten and redistribute temperature and gas. Rest lets the dough relax. Bulk is both a fermentation window and a development window.

The dough does not “know” it is supposed to take four hours. It responds to how warm it is, how much ripe starter you used, how strong the flour is, and how roughly you mixed.

## What to observe

Do not wait for a wall clock to finish. Watch the dough.

<aside class="cue">
<p><strong>Look.</strong> Volume has increased — often toward about one-and-a-half times in a moderate country dough, not a rigid doubling rule. The surface is domed and alive with bubbles under the skin, not a dry crust or a collapsed pancake.</p>
<p><strong>Feel.</strong> The mass is lighter, aerated, and still holds a shape when you fold it. It should not pour like batter unless you mixed a high-hydration dough that always does. Underfermented dough feels tight and dense. Overfermented dough feels slack, sticky, and gassy in a way that will not recover with one more fold.</p>
<p><strong>Smell.</strong> Ripe wheat and a clean lactic tang, not nail-polish solvent, and not nothing.</p>
</aside>

A finger dent that fills slowly is a clue, not a verdict. Combine it with volume, strength, and aroma. If the dough is cold from the mixer, bulk will be slow even when the inoculation is generous; if it came off the hook hot, the same formula races [src:gg:source:king-arthur-desired-dough-temperature].

## Variables

| Lever | What it usually does |
| --- | --- |
| Dough temperature | Warmer dough ferments faster. DDT is the starting condition, not a lock for the whole bulk. |
| Inoculation | More ripe starter (as a percentage of flour) shortens bulk and can push acidity earlier. |
| Flour | Enzyme-active or whole-grain flours often move faster; a very strong flour can hide gas until later. |
| Hydration | Wetter dough shows activity earlier and slacks sooner. |
| Folds | Build strength and even out temperature. They do not replace fermentation. |
| Ambient after the mix | A one-kilo dough at home drifts toward room temperature; a trough in a shop does not. |

Ranges such as “three to five hours at ~24 °C” are workshop descriptions of *a* dough, not laws. Record what *your* formula does at a measured dough temperature.

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Likely causes | Still possible? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tight, small, barely risen | Cold dough, weak starter, short time, low inoculation | Usually yes: wait, or warm slightly. Do not add water as a fermentation fix. |
| Explodes at the score, dense crumb | Shaped too soon; gas and extensibility were not there | The bake will not rescue it. Use the loaf; change the next bulk. |
| Slack, sticky, won’t hold a shape | Too far, too warm, too much acid, or hydration the flour cannot carry | A short cold rest can help *slightly*. Past a point, make focaccia, not bâtards. |
| Strong sour, weak oven spring | Long, warm bulk; high inoculation | Flavor is not a substitute for gas. Shorten bulk or cool the dough. |

Quality failure (weak crumb, poor shape) is not a food-safety failure. Do not invent “safe bulk times.” Pathogen control for other foods is a different page.

<aside class="pro-note">
<p>In production, bulk is a scheduling object. Bakers move dough temperature, inoculation, and retardation so troughs land when benches and ovens are free. Consistency across batches matters more than a romantic rise time. Measure dough temperature at the start of bulk; do not guess from the room thermostat.</p>
</aside>
