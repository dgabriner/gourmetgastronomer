Food safety is not a mood. Canning, dairy, and many ferments have failure modes that enthusiasm does not cancel. This page points at authorities. It does not invent process times.

## Canning

NCHFP, adapting USDA AIB 539: *Clostridium botulinum* can grow in moist, low-acid, low-oxygen food and produce a deadly toxin. Acid foods (pH 4.6 or below) may be boiling-water canned with a researched time. Low-acid foods need a pressure canner at 240–250 °F [src:gg:source:nchfp-ensuring-safe-canned-foods]. Details: [[gg:topic:canning]] and [[gg:topic:acidity]].

Cooking [[gg:topic:jam]] until it gels is not the same as canning it. The strawberry formula in this encyclopedia follows NCHFP, including altitude-adjusted boiling-water times [src:gg:source:nchfp-strawberry-jam-pectin]. If you change fruit, sugar, or acid, you are no longer on that process. See [[gg:recipe:strawberry-jam]].

## Dairy

U.S. cheddar identity requires that if dairy ingredients are not pasteurized, the cheese is cured at not less than 35 °F for at least 60 days [src:gg:source:cfr-21-133-113-cheddar]. That is a rule for a standardized commercial cheese, not a home-cheesemaking procedure this site will improvise. Fresh cheeses and raw milk have different, stricter practical risks. Do not invent a “safe” aging chart.

## Fermentation

Sourdough and wine are not canning. They still fail — off smells, unwanted films, exploding bottles. When a ferment is used as a *preservation* method (some pickles, some cured meats), use a tested process. This encyclopedia has not built those pages yet. Until it does, do not treat bread starter as a model for low-acid canned food.

If two authorities disagree, follow the tested process for your jurisdiction and altitude. Do not average them. [[gg:topic:preserving]] is the craft hub.
