Affinage is not a cave. It is the period after the vat when a cheese is still a living, drying, enzymatic object. FAO calls this stage ripening: biochemical change driven mainly by enzymes of bacteria (and, in many styles, of molds or yeasts on a rind) [src:gg:source:fao-small-scale-cheese]. The French word names the craft of paying attention. The physics is water leaving, proteins and fats breaking down, and surfaces either being protected or being colonized on purpose.

## What is actually changing

A young paste is a concentrated dairy gel with residual lactose, moisture, salt, and a starting pH. During aging:

- Moisture migrates and evaporates. The paste firms, or it can collapse if the rind seals too early over a wet interior.
- Residual sugars and proteins feed microbes. [[gg:topic:fermentation]] continues, slower than in a vat, often as a surface ecology rather than a bulk culture bloom.
- Proteolysis and lipolysis change texture from rubbery to creamy or crystalline and change aroma from lactic to savory, animal, or ammoniated, depending on the style.
- The [[gg:topic:cheese-rind]] is not decoration. It is a humidity and oxygen membrane, and sometimes a factory of flavor.

Wisconsin’s Center for Dairy Research treats aging as composition plus environment plus *checking the cheese*: moisture, fat, pH, and salt set the trajectory; temperature and humidity are the room; periodic grading tells you whether the trajectory is the one you wanted [src:gg:source:cdr-pipeline-2015-cheese]. Their practical warning is specific. Storing wheels for months without looking is how affineurs get catastrophic disappointment, not how flavor “develops itself.” CDR’s shop advice is to evaluate aging lots on the order of every two to three months [src:gg:source:cdr-pipeline-2015-cheese]. That interval is professional practice, not a home-kitchen clock.

## Observation over calendar

Time is a proxy. The cheese is the signal.

**Look.** Rind color, evenness, cracks, oiling, and whether bloomy coats stay white or yellow and slip. Interior: openness, mechanical holes versus fermentation eyes, mottling.

**Feel.** A paste that yields like cold butter is not the same as one that squeaks or weeps. Press the heel of a hard wheel; it should not feel hollow unless the style is supposed to be.

**Smell.** Young lactic, mushroom, washed-rind savory, ammonia. Ammonia at the surface can be a late bloomy-rind stage; ammonia as a room stench with a soapy paste is a different failure.

**Taste.** Salt should be present, not a crust. Bitterness can be proteolysis gone far or a composition that never should have been aged that long — CDR’s example is a too-moist, under-salted cheddar taken to a “sharp” calendar [src:gg:source:cdr-pipeline-2015-cheese].

## Variables

Strong levers:

- **Starting composition.** Moisture, fat, pH, and salt at demolding. You cannot affine your way out of a wet, low-salt vat if the style needed the opposite [src:gg:source:cdr-pipeline-2015-cheese].
- **Temperature.** Faster biochemistry when warmer; more risk of unwanted surface growth and oiling.
- **Humidity and airflow.** Too dry: cracked rinds, halted surface flora. Too wet: smears, stickiness, unwanted mucor or bacteria.
- **Turning, washing, brushing, wrapping.** Mechanical work that chooses which microbes win.
- **Vessel.** Open shelf, box, vacuum, or wax changes oxygen. Wine aging in oak is a cousin idea, not the same page; see [[gg:topic:wine]].

U.S. cheddar identity allows unpasteurized dairy ingredients only if the cheese is then cured at not less than 35 °F for at least 60 days [src:gg:source:cfr-21-133-113-cheddar]. That is a legal control for that standardized food, not a tasting recommendation and not a general license to age raw-milk cheese at home.

## Failure modes

- **Drying too fast.** Rind armor over a still-wet paste; later, slits or bitterness.
- **Humidity too high.** Rind slip, pink slime, or ammonia that never resolves.
- **No grading.** The room looks fine; the lot is not.
- **Confusing safety with quality.** A sealed, correctly processed commercial cheese and a forgotten tub of milk are not the same experiment.

## Professional note

In a creamery, affinage is inventory: lot codes, room maps, and a feedback loop from grader to vat [src:gg:source:cdr-pipeline-2015-cheese]. Home aging of purchased cheese is mostly storage — keep it cold enough, dry the cut face, eat it before the paste soaps. Home cheesemaking that aims at long aging is a dairy process with pathogen rules; this encyclopedia will not improvise them.
